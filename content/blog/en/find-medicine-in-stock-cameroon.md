You have a prescription in your hand, the first pharmacy says "rupture", and the second is closed. For many families in Cameroon, finding a medicine still means walking or phoning from pharmacy to pharmacy — or giving up and buying from a street stall.

This guide shows how to find a medicine in stock near you with the OpesCare **Medicine Finder**, how to read the results honestly, and why taking the extra minute to find a licensed pharmacy matters.

## Why this is harder than it should be

### Stock information does not travel

Each pharmacy knows its own shelves. Patients do not. Without a shared, up-to-date view, the only way to find out is to ask, one pharmacy at a time.

### The informal market fills the gap

When a medicine is hard to find, the informal market — the "pharmacies de rue" — is often closer and cheaper. The risks are documented:

- WHO issued an alert in 2018 about falsified Augmentin (an antibiotic) found at street-market level in Douala.
- WHO issued another alert in 2017 about falsified penicillin V tablets in Cameroon that contained paracetamol instead of the antibiotic.
- A critical review in the *American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene* found that the majority of samples in Cameroonian medicine-quality studies were collected from the informal sector.
- A peer-reviewed study carried out with Cameroon's national medicine quality control laboratory (LANACOME) reported a 7.9% prevalence of substandard or falsified ciprofloxacin and metronidazole tablets in three cities.

A falsified antibiotic does not just fail to cure. It can let an infection worsen and contribute to resistance. Making the licensed pharmacy easier to find is one practical answer.

## How the Medicine Finder works

### What you see

The [Medicine Finder](https://opescare.com/network/medicine-finder) answers one question: **which pharmacies on the OpesCare network report this medicine, and when did they last say so?**

For each result you get:

- the pharmacy's name and address;
- distance from you, if you share your location;
- a map and directions that open inside OpesCare;
- **when the stock was last confirmed** by the pharmacy;
- contact details and opening hours, where the pharmacy has provided them.

### Where the data comes from

Results come only from the **live stock records** of pharmacies on the network. Those records are kept current by the pharmacy staff at the counter as they receive and dispense medicines.

OpesCare applies a strict rule: only stock whose source is on an approved list counts as availability. Sample data, demonstration data and imported test files are never shown to the public as stock. That is why, in some towns or for some medicines, you may see **no results**. An empty result is an honest one: it means no connected pharmacy has reported that medicine. We would rather show you nothing than show you something untrue.

### What the timestamp means

A line confirmed this morning is more reliable than one confirmed three weeks ago. Use the "last updated" time to decide whether to call first. Stock moves quickly; the finder tells you what the pharmacy last said, not what is on the shelf this second.

## Step by step: finding your medicine

### 1. Open the finder

Go to [opescare.com/network/medicine-finder](https://opescare.com/network/medicine-finder) on your phone or computer, or open the OpesCare mobile app. No account is needed to search.

### 2. Type the medicine name

Type the name as it appears on your prescription. Brand names and generic names (the active ingredient) can both be useful; if one gives no result, try the other. Check the strength (for example 500 mg) and the form (tablet, syrup, injection) match your prescription.

### 3. Choose your area

Allow location to see the nearest pharmacies first, or pick your town or neighbourhood.

### 4. Read the results

Look at distance and the last-updated time together. A pharmacy two kilometres away that confirmed stock today is usually a better bet than one 500 metres away that confirmed it last month.

### 5. Confirm before you travel

Availability is **information to act on, not a reservation**. Call the pharmacy, or, where the pharmacy accepts reservations through OpesCare, reserve the medicine before you go.

### 6. Collect and keep your history

If you have a Health ID and an electronic prescription, the pharmacist can look you up by Health ID or QR, see the prescription, and record what was dispensed — fully or partially. That dispensing then appears in your history, so the next doctor knows what you actually took.

## Tips for patients

- **Never substitute on your own.** If the exact medicine is not available, ask the pharmacist or your doctor about an equivalent. Do not switch based on a search result.
- **Check the packaging.** Licensed pharmacies are your best protection, but still check that packaging is intact, labelling is legible and the expiry date has not passed.
- **Keep your prescription.** An electronic prescription on OpesCare carries a verification link, so a pharmacist can check it is genuine.
- **Ask about price.** Pharmacies on the network can publish prices in XAF; prices can differ between pharmacies.
- **In an emergency, go to a health facility.** The finder helps you plan; it does not replace urgent care.

## Reading a prescription before you search

A few details on your prescription make the search much more useful:

- **Active ingredient (generic name).** For example "amoxicillin" rather than a brand. Many brands share one active ingredient.
- **Strength.** 250 mg and 500 mg are not interchangeable without your prescriber's advice.
- **Form.** Tablets, capsules, syrup, suspension, injection — a child's syrup is not the same as an adult tablet.
- **Quantity and duration.** If a pharmacy has only part of the quantity, the pharmacist can record a partial dispensing, and you can collect the rest later or elsewhere.

If any of this is unclear, ask your prescriber or pharmacist before you search. With an electronic prescription on OpesCare, the details are typed, not handwritten, which removes one common source of confusion.

## How regulators fit in

Medicines in Cameroon are regulated under the Ministry of Public Health. The national medicine quality control laboratory, LANACOME, is a public institution based in Yaoundé whose mission includes checking the quality of imported medicines, as reported by *Cameroon Tribune* when it was reorganised. OpesCare is not a regulator and does not test medicines. What it can do is make the regulated channel — the licensed pharmacy — easier to find than the unregulated one.

## Three common situations

**"The finder shows the medicine, but the pharmacy says it is out."** Stock changes between updates. Look at the timestamp; ask the pharmacist when they expect a delivery, and check the next result.

**"Only a different brand is available."** Ask the pharmacist whether it contains the same active ingredient, strength and form. If you are unsure, check with your prescriber.

**"There are no results in my town."** Widen the area, or search by the generic name. If still nothing appears, no connected pharmacy has reported it; call pharmacies directly and encourage your local pharmacy to join the network.

## For pharmacies: being found

If you run a pharmacy, the Medicine Finder only works if your stock is visible.

### Keep your stock current

Your pharmacy dashboard on OpesCare includes stock screens: receive stock, adjust it, and let collections decrement it automatically. Each update refreshes the "last confirmed" time patients see.

A stock import tool is available. It reads CSV, Excel and OpenDocument files, lets you map your own column names, shows a preview, and only commits lines after you confirm. Imported lines become ordinary stock movements with a full trail.

### Publish prices and services

A whole-franc price list with history, opening hours, a photo and your map location make your listing more useful and help patients choose.

## What the Medicine Finder does not do

- It does **not** sell medicines or take payment for them.
- It does **not** guarantee stock or hold medicines for you unless the pharmacy confirms a reservation.
- It does **not** list street vendors or unverified sellers.
- It does **not** give medical advice. Your doctor and pharmacist do.
